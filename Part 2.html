How roles change and how to run the organization around them

Part 1 argued that strategy, structure and the use of released capacity need settling before any role changes. This part covers the roles themselves: the method for changing them, what it looks like in an IT service, how to manage the organization once they have changed, and how to keep redesign going as AI capability moves on.

AuthorsChris Colligan, Emily Lansdowne, Paul Veiga and Vinu Cherian

▶Where Part 1 left off

Role redesign sits in steps 4 and 5 of a six-step sequence

Part 1 set out a six-step sequence for bringing AI into an organization. The first three steps settle what the business needs, how work is organized, and where the time AI releases will go. Roles change in steps 4 and 5, once those are in place.

  1. Strategy. What the business needs to achieve.
  2. Structure. How the operating model organizes work, ownership and value streams to deliver it.
  3. Capacity released. The human time AI frees up, and where leaders decide to put it.
  4. Role changes. How roles and skills change around the new work. Covered in this part.
  5. Reskilling. The training, practice and support people need to perform the reshaped role. Covered in this part.
  6. Management. How the operating model is now run: decision rights, accountability, measures and teams.
Our view

Role redesign is an operating model decision, and it should follow from strategy and structure rather than from whichever tools arrive first. The CEO and COO set the operating model and business unit leaders run the work it changes. Inside it, the CIO and the CHRO shape the role decisions together; neither can make them alone.

01How roles change

Roles change by design, guided by a method

This section goes inside steps 4 and 5 of the six-step sequence: role changes and reskilling. Once strategy, structure and capacity are clear, role choices follow a defined method. Every role change should trace back to a decision someone made, and be explainable to the person whose job it is.

Most of the change happens in the Reconstruct work step. Once a job is broken into tasks, leaders decide what happens to each part of the work. We call this Adopt & Adapt, and it gives four options: retire the work, reshape it, reinforce it or build something new. The skills inside each role shift with those choices.

02What it looks like in practice

Same titles, different jobs

The same shift is happening across knowledge work. Analysts, engineers, underwriters and planners are moving from producing work to directing it, checking it and owning the outcome. IT service management shows it most clearly: demand falls fast once agents arrive, and the economics are easy to see.

One role up close: the service desk analyst

Today

Most of their time goes on structured, repeatable cases: password resets, access requests, known errors. They find the resolution path, escalate where needed, and log the outcome. Their value is throughput. Their expertise builds through repetition.

With agents handling the routine load

  • Define the outcomes agents should achieve, and when a case should escalate.
  • Curate the knowledge agents resolve from.
  • Handle the exceptions, which are now harder on average.
  • Close the learning loop by reviewing what agents did, and why.

This is a more skilled role than the one it replaces.

03Managing the new organization

These are not just HR decisions; they are operating model decisions

Redesigned roles need a redesigned organization around them. The CHRO and CIO shape these choices, but most of them land in structures, budgets and measures that the CEO, COO and business unit leaders own. They cannot be settled in HR or IT alone. Most of the organizations we work with are still working this part out.

Managing people and agents

Team leads now allocate work between people and agents, and own agent cost and risk alongside team health. Span of control should be set by how much work one person can properly review, rather than by headcount.

Decision rights by risk

Routine, reversible decisions can move to agents or to the person closest to the work. Irreversible ones stay with named people. See Figure 4 below.

Measures that follow value

Throughput and tickets closed stop meaning much when agents can produce volume around the clock. Measure outcomes, quality and cost per unit of work instead. See Figure 5 below.

Contracts and sourcing

Supplier agreements and service levels written around human effort need revisiting as demand falls. Build, buy or partner choices also shape which skills stay inside and which oversight roles must be staffed.

Funding the recurring costs

Oversight, reskilling and agent run costs continue for as long as the agents run. They belong in the business case next to the savings.

Assets with named owners

The knowledge base, the agent platform and the evaluation sets are assets the service depends on. Name an owner for each, with a budget and service levels of its own.

Grading, measures and career paths

A reshaped role that asks for more skill than the one it replaced needs regrading, new performance measures and a career path. Without those, people do more skilled work on the old terms.

We most often see redesign stall on decision rights and measures. If nobody has the authority to stop an agent, and people are still judged on volume, the reshaped roles cannot work as designed. Figures 4 and 5 show what to change.

04Make redesign a discipline

Treat the first design as a hypothesis to test

Role redesign is often run as a project with a go-live date, but AI capability changes too quickly for that. What agents can do in twelve months will not be what they can do today, and the operating model that makes sense now will need revisiting.

A shared responsibility with the answer where they meet

The value from agents arrives when the organization changes how it operates to match what the technology can do. Speed of deployment matters far less.

The CIO brings the technology view, the CHRO the people view. They shape role redesign together, inside an operating model the CEO and COO set and business unit leaders run. The answer sits where all of them meet.
